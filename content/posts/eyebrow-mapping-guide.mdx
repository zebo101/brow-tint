Eyebrow mapping is a way to plan where your brows begin, where their arches sit, and where their tails end. It gives you reference points to compare with your existing brows before deciding on a shape. You can use the same idea on a photo, without changing your actual eyebrows.

The start, arch, and end are also the three areas described in [Benefit's explanation of brow mapping](https://www.benefitcosmetics.com/services.html). Different methods use slightly different facial reference lines; a map is a starting point for a decision, rather than a single compulsory outline.

## What do the three brow points mean?

| Point | What it describes                   | What to look at on your photo                                         |
| ----- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Head  | The inner beginning of the brow     | Where your existing hairs start and the space between your brows      |
| Arch  | The higher part of the brow contour | Its position along the brow and its height relative to the other side |
| Tail  | The outer end of the brow           | Its length, direction, and position relative to your eye              |

There are three points on each side, giving you six points in total. Check the whole face as well as the close-up. A point that looks slightly uneven when enlarged may feel balanced at normal viewing size.

## How mapping points relate to face proportions

A useful map keeps the brows in context with the eyes and the rest of the face. Rather than looking for one ideal set of distances, ask how each point relates to the features already visible in your photo. Separate placement questions from style preferences before moving anything.

- **Head and spacing:** Compare each inner point with the start of your brow hairs and the space above the bridge of your nose. Moving the heads inward changes that gap even if the arches stay still. Check both sides together instead of using a dark patch of makeup as the only boundary.
- **Arch and direction:** Notice both where the arch sits along the brow and how high it rises. Moving it outward changes the lengths of the sections before and after it; moving it upward changes the rise. Decide which of those differences you actually want to test.
- **Tail and length:** View the tail alongside the outer eye corner and your existing hair coverage. Check its direction as well as its endpoint. A longer tail is not automatically a better fit, especially if it takes the proposed contour far beyond the brow you started with.

For style ideas, use the [eyebrow shape for face shape guide](/eyebrow-shape-for-face-shape). Judge your own face shape: Browlens does not detect or classify it. Classic face-shape suggestions can help you select a first outline, but they are starting points, not rules that override your preferences or natural brow placement.

## What does the difference report tell you?

The report measures differences visible in your photo. It can help you notice that one arch sits higher or one brow extends farther. Values are measured in photo pixels, so they are not millimeter measurements and should not be compared directly across differently sized photos.

Use the numbers to investigate a detail, not to chase a zero score. Browlens does not give a beauty rating or require perfectly matching sides. Check the original photo alongside the guide and keep the features you like.

## Common mapping mistakes and photo checks

Before refining the map, check whether you are correcting a brow detail or compensating for the photograph. These checks help separate the two:

- **Mapping a tilted head or raised brow.** Look at the eye line and your expression in the original. If one side is raised because you tilted your head or lifted an eyebrow, take another relaxed, front-facing photo before trying to level the guide points.
- **Following shadow instead of hair.** Zoom in around the brow head and tail to check whether a dark area is hair, makeup, or lighting. If the edge is unclear, use a more evenly lit photo rather than guessing where it ends.
- **Moving the wrong part of the arch.** If you want the bend to happen farther along the brow, lifting its point may only make it taller. Make a small change in one direction, then compare the full contour with your original before continuing.
- **Copying one side without checking the other.** Inspect each brow against its own eye and hair coverage. Matching point heights alone can hide a difference in placement. View the full portrait before deciding that a visible difference needs an edit.

For a final check, follow each brow from head to arch to tail on the original photo, then repeat on the edited outline. Ask whether every point is intentional and whether the connecting contour still follows the direction you wanted. If not, revisit that point rather than changing all six.
